I measured how well word-level timestamps from speech recognition locate a sentence inside a video. Got 0.60 seconds of mean error, segment IoU of 0.73, and wrote a nice paragraph about how a perfect transcript still isn't a perfect citation.

Then a test failed. It feeds the metric an oracle transcript — my own authored text with my own authored timings, zero error by construction — and asserts it scores about zero. It scored 0.64 seconds.

Two things were wrong, and neither would ever have crashed. One of them: the metric picked which hypothesis words to score by checking whether their midpoint fell within a second of the authored sentence, so it was using the predicted timings to decide which timings to measure. That's circular. And one second is wider than the 0.45 second gap between my sentences, so every measurement was quietly swallowing words from its neighbours and inflating every span.

Align by text instead (Levenshtein backtrace, so timings play no part in choosing what to measure) and it's 0.15 seconds mean error, IoU 0.88 to 0.93. My real answer was four times better than the one I'd written a paragraph about.

The assertion that caught it is embarrassingly small: give the metric a perfect input, demand a perfect score. No ground truth corpus, no labelling, just a control you build yourself. I'd been running the system for weeks without it.

What I'm building

An engine that turns a long video into something you can ask questions of. Upload a two-hour lecture, ask when backprop was explained or which bits show a red car, get an answer with the timestamps and frames behind it.

┌──────────── INGESTION · throughput-bound, has to fit in 16 GB ──────────┐ video ─▶ DECODE ─▶ FRAME SELECT ─┬─▶ vision encoder ─┐ M0 M4 ├─▶ ASR + timestamps├─▶ TEMPORAL INDEX └─▶ OCR ────────────┘ M6 └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ ┌──────────────────── QUERY · latency-bound ──────────────────────────────┐ question ─▶ RETRIEVE ─▶ FUSE ─▶ TEMPORAL RERANK ─▶ evidence ─▶ VLM M6/M7 M7 M8 M9 answer └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Two systems in one repo with opposite constraints. Ingestion is a batch job that mustn't blow past 16 GB; query is interactive and has a latency budget. I keep them apart on purpose because every time I've seen them conflated the result optimises for neither.

The shortcut that works on your test file

My first Frame object carried an index, and time was computed as index / fps wherever it was needed. Simpler, no time-base arithmetic, and on my test video the drift against the container's own timestamps measured 0.00 ms. Exactly right.

It's exactly right for constant frame rate video and nothing else. Screen recorders emit frames when the screen changes, so a QuickTime or Zoom capture at "30 fps" is an average and index-derived time drifts without bound. The whole deliverable here is a timestamp somebody clicks. So the failure mode is a confident citation pointing at the wrong moment, and no test catches it unless the test checks the number.

Time comes from the container's presentation timestamps now, and there's a test asserting the two agree on a file I know is constant-rate. When that one eventually fails it'll be telling me something true about the input.

I don't have a tidy rule for spotting this class in advance. Best I've got is: ask what property of the input makes the shortcut valid, then go and check whether real inputs have it. If the answer is "sometimes", write the assertion while you still remember why you care.

Cheap, plausible, and wrong

Encoders insert a keyframe at scene cuts. A keyframe scan costs 0.03× a full decode. So a nearly-free keyframe scan should hand my shot detector its boundaries, right?

Because I authored the test video I could actually check. x264 found one boundary out of four. It broke its regular three-second cadence exactly once, at the hard cut. Its dissolve produced no keyframe at all, and two of the "boundaries" only look found because the cadence happened to land near them.

So keyframes are high precision and low recall: a broken cadence means something, silence means nothing. Fine as a prior, useless as an answer. Had I not checked, it would have quietly capped the detector's recall forever and I'd have spent the time tuning thresholds instead.

The bit I found more interesting is which boundary the encoder missed. It was the dissolve, and commercial scene-cut heuristics fail on dissolves too, for the same reason mine does — the change per frame is genuinely small. When a well-tuned production system fails the way yours does, the difficulty is in the problem, and that's worth knowing before you spend a week assuming you're just bad at this.

When the prose and the numbers disagree

I shipped a per-modality table with a hand-written verdict column saying that visual and on-screen-text questions were impossible for a speech-only system. That same table printed hit@5 of 1.000 for both. I'd written the verdict before running the measurement and it did not survive.

Both were true, about different things. Retrieval really does find the right moment for "what colour is the object that crosses the screen", because the narration says an object travels across the screen — it just never says the colour. Localisation succeeded. Answerability failed. Nothing in my harness could tell those apart.

There's a metric for the second one now: does the retrieved text actually contain the answer. It reads 0.778 for speech questions and exactly 0.000 for every other type. That's what the prose had been trying to claim without ever measuring it.

Every time my writing and my numbers have disagreed on this project, the answer has been neither "fix the sentence" nor "fix the code". I was measuring the wrong quantity.

The benchmark needed its own tests

My temporal questions scored hit@5 of 1.000. So did random retrieval. Exactly 1.000.

That one's my fault from authoring time. Questions like "which is discussed first, embeddings or containers?" got ground-truth intervals running from one shot to nearly the end of the video. Any chunk overlaps an interval that wide, so everything counts as correct and the question measures nothing at all.

Both questions are still in the eval set, reported next to their random baseline instead of quietly dropped. A question that measures nothing is only instructive if you can see it doing that. My assertion is a property of the eval set and not of the system: chance performance on a question has to come in below some threshold.

One measurement is an anecdote

Having measured embedding contrast at 512 dimensions — uniform 0.088, clustered 0.300, low_rank 0.382 — the ordering looked like a law, so I asserted it in a test. It failed immediately at 256 dimensions, where clustered (0.421) goes past low_rank (0.344).

Only "uniform is worst" was ever a theoretical statement. The rest was a coincidence of whichever settings I happened to run first, because the two regimes are controlled by different parameters and they don't scale with dimension the same way.

Now when a pattern in my results starts feeling like a law, I go and vary the thing I wasn't varying. Usually that's a parameter sweep inside the test, which costs about four lines.

Sometimes it really is the environment

Two of these cost an afternoon between them and had nothing to do with machine learning.

Newest Python is reliably the worst choice for an ML project: wheel availability lags releases by six to twelve months and the long tail lags further. Pinning one minor version back turned a from-source build into an install that finished in one go.

Better story: the interpreter just died. Tests aborted during a torch import, but only when two particular test files ran in the same process. Two copies of OpenMP in the virtualenv — one from torch, one from the vector index library — and the second runtime to initialise kills the process. Import order doesn't help, because the abort happens on initialisation, so reordering only changes which library dies. I checked both directions before accepting that.

My only workaround is an environment flag whose own documentation says it may silently produce incorrect results. So instead of deciding it was probably fine, there's now a test making all three libraries compute the same top-k in one process and asserting they agree. If the flag ever does the thing the docs warn about, something fails loudly. I still don't love it.

The same bug wearing a different hat

An intrinsic dimension estimator reported 0.3. There's no such thing as a fractional intrinsic dimension below one, so the estimator was broken.

It assumes points come from a continuous density, so no two coincide. Video violates that comprehensively — consecutive frames of a static slide sit about a millionth of a unit apart, one ratio in the estimator hit 345,901, and a handful of points dominated the whole sum. 75 of 89 frames had a near twin.

I'd already found and fixed this exact hazard once, in a different diagnostic, where the symptom was infinity instead of 0.3. Fixing one instance of a bug class does not fix the class, apparently, and I needed to be shown that twice. Probably the useful outcome wasn't the patch — it was promoting near-duplicate fraction to a first-class diagnostic, because the thing that broke the estimator turns out to be one of the most important facts about the data.

Why write any of it from scratch

Four components here are written explicitly and then measured against the production alternative behind the same interface: a mini-CLIP, the nearest neighbour index built up from brute force through IVF to HNSW by hand, a shot detector, and a spatiotemporal video encoder.

Those scratch versions lose. They're meant to. I build them because I can't really reason about a retrieval stack I've never implemented, and every failure above came from reasoning about the stack, not from writing a layer. The value showed up in the diagnostics and the eval set, which are the parts nobody hands you.

My decision log and the full failure catalogue, with dates and numbers, are in the repo under docs/. There are more than the ones here.
