Three thousand steps in, mel L1 loss had fallen from 0.81 to 0.24, smoothly, no spikes. That's the shape of curve you screenshot and feel good about.

The model had learned essentially nothing. L1 falls early just by predicting blurry average speech, so the curve was measuring the wrong thing entirely. The free-running attention map was a smear with no diagonal in it. Generation cut out at 2.2 seconds on an 82-character sentence. Running the audio back through Whisper, one half-sentence came out correct and the rest was noise.

For a sequence-to-sequence synthesizer, the instrument is the alignment map, not the loss. A diagonal forming means it's working. A smear means it isn't, and the loss won't tell you which one you've got.

Why this direction is harder

I'd assumed synthesis would be the easy way round. It isn't, and why not is specific enough to be worth a whole post.

In recognition, the alignment between audio frames and characters exists. It's hidden in the signal, and CTC's trick is to never commit to it — sum over every possible alignment instead. In synthesis there's no alignment to find. You have 19 characters, you need to produce roughly 250 mel frames, and nothing anywhere tells you it should be 250.

RecognitionSynthesis

That last row is everything. CTC can stay agnostic because it only ever scores a target you already hold. A synthesizer is producing the output, so at every step it has to commit: is the "o" in "go" done, or does it hold for another 40 ms? Get that wrong enough and you don't get a slightly wrong transcript. You get speech that stutters, skips a syllable, or never stops.

The four tricks, and what happened without one

Tacotron's answer is to generate mel frames one step at a time and use attention over the text at every step to ask "which character am I on right now?". The attention is the alignment, built as it goes.

Four things make that trainable. Teacher forcing feeds the true previous frame during training, because otherwise early training compounds its own garbage. Reduction factor r=2 predicts two frames per step, halving the sequence attention has to traverse. Location-sensitive attention pushes the previous alignment through a convolution into the scorer, a soft hint saying "you were at character 12, look near 13" — content-only attention stuttered and skipped.

The fourth one is the one I'd have removed if I hadn't tried it. The prenet's dropout stays on at inference, and that looks like a bug you'd fix. It's there because the previous-frame path is so informative the decoder will happily copy it forward and ignore the text completely. Turn it off and you get fluent, confident mumble, totally uncorrelated with what you asked for. I did turn it off, once, and it sounds exactly as strange as that description suggests.

A prior, not more data

With 19 training utterances the attention never had enough signal to work out on its own that text is read left to right. So the fix isn't more data, it's telling it.

Guided attention adds a penalty mean(A · W) where W[t,n] = 1 − exp(−(n/N − t/T)² / 2g²), taxing attention mass far from the diagonal and leaving mass near it free. It's a nudge, not a constraint, and with enough data you'd drop it. Alignment then formed in hundreds of steps instead of never.

Judge it with the other model you already built

Mel L1 says nothing about intelligibility and I don't have a listening panel. What I do have is a speech recognizer, so the synthesizer gets scored by transcribing its output and measuring character error against the text it was asked to say.

That judge picked step 8,000 of a 10,000 step run, at character error 0.61. It also oscillated between 0.96 and 0.61 across the run with three separate regressions, while training loss slid steadily downward the entire time. Taking the final checkpoint would have shipped something noticeably worse for no reason except that it came last.

Two clever ideas that measured worse

When attention stalls at inference, the obvious fix is restricting it to a window around wherever it was last. I implemented that. It measured worse. The model was trained with a full softmax over the whole text, so renormalising inside a window at decode time hands it context vectors of a kind it's never seen. Exposure-bias fixes belong in training, not decoding, and my clever idea was worth exactly one measurement.

Second one's about hardware. The TTS decoder is a Python loop over timesteps, each doing small tensor ops. On Apple's MPS backend it ran 13× slower than on CPU, because per-dispatch overhead dominates when the ops are tiny and strictly sequential. Accelerators buy throughput. They don't buy latency on small sequential work, and a voice agent is mostly small sequential work.

The ceiling more data can't lift

Computing a log-mel throws the phase away. Griffin-Lim gets a phase back by exploiting frame overlap — guess, inverse transform, re-measure, keep the magnitude, repeat, about 60 times. After that magnitude and phase are self-consistent.

Self-consistent isn't correct. Lots of phase fields are consistent with the same magnitude spectrogram, and the one Griffin-Lim lands on generally isn't the one the speaker produced. That mismatch is the phasey, metallic, slightly underwater quality of every sample it makes.

Everywhere else on this project, more data helped. Here it can't, at any quantity, because the limitation isn't in a learned component. Griffin-Lim has zero parameters. Train the acoustic model on ten thousand hours and the output's still phasey. That's why replacing it was worth doing before collecting any more speech.

My metric was rigged for my baseline

So I trained a neural vocoder and scored it against Griffin-Lim on multi-resolution STFT distance. Griffin-Lim won.

It won because multi-resolution STFT is a magnitude metric, and magnitude consistency is literally the objective Griffin-Lim optimises. I'd picked a scoring function that measures the one thing my baseline is built to maximise, and then believed the answer. Adding SI-SDR, time-domain and so phase-sensitive, flipped it immediately: the neural model was 5.4 dB better.

Both numbers were correct. Only one was measuring what I cared about.

Making a waveform was never the hard part. The inverse STFT does it exactly, for free, and has done since 1977. Knowing the phase was the hard part. So predict phase, run the exact inverse, and stop paying a network to rediscover arithmetic.

log-mel (B, T, 80) │ ▼ Conv1d(80 → d, k=7) + LayerNorm ▼ N × ConvNeXt block depthwise k=7 → LN → Linear(d,3d) → GELU → Linear(3d,d) ▼ Linear(d → 2·257) split: log-magnitude │ phase angle ▼ S = exp(mag) · e^(i·phase) ▼ exact inverse STFT overlap-add, nothing learned waveform

No attention in it at all. A vocoder needs a receptive field of a few tens of milliseconds, enough to place a glottal pulse, not a sentence. It also shares the recognizer's STFT grid exactly — n_fft 512, window 400, hop 160 — so the mels the acoustic model already emits drop straight in with no retraining.

That grid agreement isn't a detail. Reusing a 512/256 overlap-add shell for a denoiser trained at 512/128 looked completely harmless and took 19 dB of signal to noise down to 6. Framing parameters are part of the model, not tuning knobs.

Recording an hour of my own voice

Getting a synthesizer to answer in my voice is mostly a data-collection problem, and it's the one step here you can't parallelise, buy or download. An hour of speech is around 900 takes. At ten seconds of friction per take that's two and a half hours of pure overhead. That's roughly how voice datasets end up abandoned.

So the recording tool is the thing worth building. Press return, read the sentence, stop talking, and the VAD from the recognition half ends the take. No second keypress. Every take gets checked before it counts — clipping, noise floor, silence padding, and whether the transcription matches what was on screen — because catching a bad take costs five seconds now and a day after training.

Can a model tell whose voice it is?

The last module asks that, and it's where I had to be most careful about claiming anything. It trains a classifier over training speakers, but the number it reports is equal error rate on speakers it has never heard, scored by cosine distance on a fixed trial list. Deliberately different tasks: training accuracy hitting 100% means it memorised 32 voices and says nothing about whether the embedding space generalises.

The held-out split leaves 8 speakers. An equal error rate measured on 8 speakers has a bootstrap confidence interval several points wide — wider than most of the differences I wanted to test, like angular margin against plain softmax. So the run prints the interval, the median across evaluations, and the best checkpoint separately, so I can't quietly confuse them.

On that split, the honest answer to "does the angular margin help?" is that this experiment can't tell you. Which is not the answer I wanted after training it, and is probably the most useful thing I learned building either half.
