The first half of this voice agent turns audio into text. This half turns text back into audio, and I expected it to be the easier direction. It is not, and the reason is specific enough to be worth the whole post.

In recognition, the alignment between audio frames and characters exists. It is hidden in the signal, and CTC's trick is to avoid committing to it by summing over every possible alignment. In synthesis there is no alignment to find. You have 19 characters and you need to produce roughly 250 mel frames, and nothing anywhere tells you it should be 250. The model has to invent the correspondence and then commit to it, one step at a time.

RecognitionSynthesis

That last row is the entire difficulty. CTC can stay agnostic because it only ever scores a target you already hold. A synthesizer is producing the output, so at every step it must answer: is the "o" in "go" finished, or does it hold for another 40 ms? Get that wrong repeatedly and you do not get a slightly wrong transcript. You get speech that stutters, skips a syllable, or never stops.

1Two stages, two different problems

"the old man's eyes" ~19 characters │ ▼ acoustic model learned: text is a sequence problem log-mel spectrogram (T×80) ~250 frames, and who decides T? │ ▼ vocoder signal problem: invert a lossy transform waveform @ 16 kHz ~40,000 samples

The split is worth keeping because the two halves fail differently and improve independently. Text to mel is about durations, prosody and pronunciation. Mel to waveform is about inverting a transform that threw information away. Historically they advanced separately, and the interface between them is exactly the log-mel I had already built for the recognizer. Modern systems fuse the stages for quality, but you cannot see why fusing helps until you have felt the seam.

2Four tricks, and what happens without them

Tacotron's answer to the duration problem is to generate mel frames one step at a time and use attention over the text at every step to answer "which character am I on right now". The attention is the alignment, being built as it goes rather than discovered after the fact.

every decoder step (= r mel frames): 1 prenet(previous frame) "what did I just say?" 2 attention over encoder states "which characters am I on?" ← the core 3 GRU state update "remember where I am" 4 project [state, context] → r×80 "paint the next slice of mel" 5 stop head → P(done) "am I finished talking?"

Four things make that trainable, and I only understood three of them properly after removing one and listening to the result.

Teacher forcing feeds the true previous frame during training instead of the model's own output, because otherwise early training compounds its own garbage. It is also the direct cause of the exposure-bias problem later. Reduction factor r=2 predicts two frames per step, which halves the sequence the attention has to traverse and visibly speeds up alignment forming. Location-sensitive attention feeds the previous alignment through a convolution into the scorer, a soft hint that says "you were at character 12, look near 13"; content-only attention stuttered and skipped.

The fourth is the one worth remembering. The prenet's dropout stays on at inference time, which looks like a bug. It is there because the previous-frame path is so informative that the decoder will happily copy it forward and ignore the text entirely. Without that dropout the model produces fluent, confident mumble, completely uncorrelated with what it was asked to say.

3The loss fell and the model learned nothing

First real attempt: 3,000 steps, no attention prior. Mel L1 loss fell smoothly from 0.81 to 0.24, which is exactly the shape of a curve you screenshot and feel good about.

What the curve could not see L1 falls early just by predicting blurry average speech. The free-running attention map was a smear with no diagonal in it. Generation stopped at 2.2 seconds on an 82-character sentence. Running the output through Whisper, one half-sentence came back correct and the rest was nothing.

The diagnosis was that with 19 training utterances the attention never had enough signal to discover on its own that text is read left to right. The correct instrument for a sequence-to-sequence synthesizer is not the loss curve, it is the alignment map. A diagonal forming is the model working. A smear is the model failing, and the loss will not tell you which one you have.

The fix is a prior rather than more data. Guided attention adds a penalty mean(A · W) where W[t,n] = 1 − exp(−(n/N − t/T)² / 2g²), so attention mass far from the diagonal is taxed and mass near it is free. It is a nudge, not a constraint, and with enough data you would drop it. Alignment then formed in hundreds of steps instead of never.

4Judge it with the other model you already built

Mel L1 does not tell you whether speech is intelligible, and I have no listening panel. What I do have is a speech recognizer, so the synthesizer gets scored by transcribing its output and measuring character error against the text it was asked to say.

That judge picked step 8,000 of a 10,000 step run, at character error 0.61. It also oscillated between 0.96 and 0.61 across the run with three separate regressions, while the training loss slid downward the whole time. Selecting the final checkpoint would have shipped a materially worse model for no reason other than it being last.

5Two clever ideas that measured worse

When attention stalls at inference, the obvious fix is to restrict it to a window around where it was last time. I implemented that and it measured worse. The model was trained with a full softmax over the whole text, so renormalising inside a window at decode time hands it context vectors of a kind it has never seen. Exposure-bias fixes belong in training, not decoding, and my clever idea was worth exactly one measurement.

The second one is about hardware. The TTS decoder is a Python loop over timesteps, each doing small tensor operations. On Apple's MPS backend it ran 13 times slower than on CPU, because per-dispatch overhead dominates when the operations are tiny and strictly sequential. Accelerators buy throughput. They do not buy latency on small sequential work, and a voice agent is mostly small sequential work.

6The ceiling that more data cannot lift

Computing a log-mel throws the phase away. Griffin-Lim gets a phase back by exploiting frame overlap: guess, inverse transform, re-measure, keep the magnitude, repeat, about 60 times. After that the magnitude and phase are self-consistent.

Self-consistent is not correct. Many phase fields are consistent with the same magnitude spectrogram, and the one Griffin-Lim converges to is generally not the one the speaker produced. That mismatch is the phasey, metallic, slightly underwater quality of every sample it produces.

Why this gap is different from the others Everywhere else in this project, more data helped. Here it cannot, at any quantity, because the limitation is not in a learned component. Griffin-Lim has zero parameters. Train the acoustic model on ten thousand hours and the output is still phasey. That is what made replacing it the highest-value single change available, and why it was worth doing before collecting any more speech.

7The metric was rigged in the baseline's favour

So I trained a neural vocoder and scored it against Griffin-Lim on multi-resolution STFT distance. Griffin-Lim won.

It won because multi-resolution STFT is a magnitude metric, and magnitude consistency is literally the objective Griffin-Lim optimises. I had picked a scoring function that measures the one thing my baseline is built to maximise, and then believed the result. Adding SI-SDR, which is time-domain and therefore phase-sensitive, reversed it immediately: the neural model was 5.4 dB better.

Both numbers were correct. Only one of them was measuring the thing I actually cared about, which was whether the output sounds like a person.

The insight is that the hard part was never making a waveform. The inverse STFT does that exactly and for free, and has since 1977. The hard part was only ever knowing the phase. So predict the phase, run the exact inverse, and stop paying a network to rediscover arithmetic.

log-mel (B, T, 80) │ ▼ Conv1d(80 → d, k=7) + LayerNorm ▼ N × ConvNeXt block depthwise k=7 → LN → Linear(d,3d) → GELU → Linear(3d,d) ▼ Linear(d → 2·257) split: log-magnitude │ phase angle ▼ S = exp(mag) · e^(i·phase) ▼ exact inverse STFT overlap-add, nothing learned waveform

There is no attention in it. A vocoder needs a receptive field of a few tens of milliseconds, enough to place a glottal pulse, not a sentence. It also shares the recognizer's STFT grid exactly, n_fft 512, window 400, hop 160, so the mels the acoustic model already emits drop straight in with no retraining.

That grid agreement is not a detail. Reusing a 512/256 overlap-add shell for a denoiser trained at 512/128 looked harmless and took 19 dB of signal to noise down to 6. Framing parameters are part of the model, not tuning knobs.

9Your own voice, and whether a model can tell

Getting a synthesizer to answer in my voice is mostly a data-collection problem, and it is the one step in the whole project that cannot be parallelised, bought or downloaded. An hour of speech is around 900 takes. At ten seconds of friction per take that is two and a half hours of pure overhead, which is how voice datasets end up abandoned.

So the recording tool is the artifact worth building. Press return, read the sentence, stop talking, and the voice activity detector from the recognition half ends the take. No second keypress. Every take is checked before it counts, for clipping, noise floor, silence padding, and whether the transcription matches what was on screen, because catching a bad take costs five seconds now and a day after training.

The last module asks a different question: can a model tell whose voice it is at all? It trains a classifier over training speakers, but the number it reports is equal error rate on speakers it has never heard, scored by cosine distance on a fixed trial list. Those are deliberately different tasks. Training accuracy reaching 100% means it memorised 32 voices and says nothing about whether the embedding space generalises.

It is also where I had to be most careful about claiming anything. The held-out split leaves 8 speakers, and an equal error rate measured on 8 speakers has a bootstrap confidence interval several points wide, wider than most of the differences I wanted to test, such as angular margin against plain softmax. The run prints the interval, the median across evaluations, and the best checkpoint separately, so the three cannot be quietly confused. On that split the honest answer to "does the angular margin help" is that this experiment cannot tell you.

Which is the summary of both halves of this project, really. Most of what I learned was not how to build the models. It was how to find out whether they work.
