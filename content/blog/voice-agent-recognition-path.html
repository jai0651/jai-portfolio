My speech recognizer found a way to score a perfectly respectable loss while emitting absolutely nothing.

CTC lets a model output a blank symbol at any frame it's unsure about. Mine worked out that blank on every frame is a safe answer with a finite loss, and settled there. 100% of frames blank. Output entropy 0.82 out of a possible 6.93. Greedy decoding returns the empty string, every time.

What makes that worth writing down isn't the bug. It's that both numbers I was watching were blind to it. The loss sat flat at a plausible value, which reads exactly like a plateau. Word error rate was pinned at 1.0, and that's what you'd see from a model that simply hasn't learned yet. So the instruments said "be patient" and I was patient, for longer than I'd like to admit.

So the fix isn't a better loss curve, it's a different instrument. Log the fraction of frames that come out blank. One line, and it goes straight to 1.0 the moment the collapse starts.

Four problems in a row

Listening looks like one pipeline and really isn't.

Denoising is signal processing. VAD is binary classification. Endpointing is policy and has no model in it anywhere. Recognition is sequence transduction with an alignment problem underneath. Every time I got properly stuck it was because I'd picked up the tool that belongs to the box next door.

The VAD was a data problem wearing a model's clothes

My voice activity detector is 55,000 parameters, small enough that you can't really blame the architecture for anything. It was confidently wrong in the most ordinary situation there is: room noise, nobody talking, and it reported P(speech) = 1.0.

Same architecture, same training loop, fixed the training data, and that same input scores 0.04. Nothing was wrong with the model. What I'd shown it was wrong.

Cheapest thing I did on this project was writing the eval set before training anything: digital silence, mains hum, babble, speech recorded too quietly. Took ten minutes. It then decided which checkpoint shipped, because the best hard-case score was 0.806 at step 2750 while the final step of the run scored 0.699. Last isn't best, and nothing except a targeted eval set was ever going to tell me that.

The one box you shouldn't put a model in

Endpointing answers "have they finished talking?" and I think adding a model there would be a mistake. It's a state machine over VAD output with a hangover timer, and all the interesting content is in the policy — how long a pause runs before it counts as a turn ending, and what happens to that threshold mid-sentence.

It also decides the threading model for everything else, which I didn't see coming. Barge-in means detecting speech while the agent is still talking. VAD is sub-millisecond so it can sit inline, but recognition and synthesis take hundreds of milliseconds, and if either runs on the event loop your agent literally can't hear you while it speaks. The architecture of everything else falls out of that one requirement.

The alignment problem, and the trick that ducks it

Here's the core difficulty. You have 400 audio frames and three characters, and nothing tells you which frames belong to which character. Hand-labelling that at any scale isn't happening.

CTC's answer is to refuse the question. Define a many-to-one collapse — merge repeats, drop blanks — then sum the probability of every frame labelling that collapses to your target, and a forward-backward recursion makes that sum tractable. That blank does two jobs that are easy to run together: it lets the model say nothing at a frame it's unsure about, and it separates genuine repeats, because without a blank between them "hello" collapses to "helo".

My first recognizer, a BiGRU with a CTC head on real LibriSpeech, produced this:

Word error rate 1.076, so worse than emitting nothing. But look at what kind of wrong it is. Rhythm's roughly right. The syllable count's roughly right. What's missing is any sense that English words are a finite set — and CTC assumes conditional independence between frames given the audio, and that is precisely a statement that it has no language model. That's what acoustics sound like without one.

Two more that trained happily while throwing away my work

PyTorch's CTC loss with zero_infinity=True substitutes a zero gradient when a target is longer than the encoder output. The sample becomes impossible instead of loud, so a bad vocabulary choice silently trains on a fraction of your data.

And torch.cuda.is_available() returned True twice on machines where a convolution then failed — once with a CUDA 13 wheel against a CUDA 12 driver, once with cuDNN 9.20 against a 9.10 driver. Asking whether a capability exists isn't the same as exercising it. My bootstrap runs an actual convolution now and looks at the result.

It was the warmup, and I nearly never found out

After the collapse I had a confident theory. Subword vocabulary of 1024, too large for the data, restart at 256. I was about to spend three days on it.

Instead I spent an hour reading the reference recipe. ESPnet uses 5,000 subwords on this same corpus and gets 8.4% word error. So vocabulary wasn't the problem, and here's the part that bothers me: if I'd "fixed" it that way and it had happened to work, I'd have learned something false and kept it permanently.

Actual cause was the learning rate schedule. My warmup was 1,500 steps against the reference's 15,000. Too fast a rise locks CTC into the all-blank minimum and it doesn't come back out.

Worth a look at where the parameters actually sit, because it isn't where the name suggests. Two thirds of each block is feed-forward. Attention is the smallest learned part of the thing.

Two heads on one shared encoder, because each covers the other's structural weakness. CTC is monotonic, cheap, streams trivially, and has no language model — that's where "mianthes" comes from. An attention decoder has the language model and can also wander or loop, since nothing forces its attention forward. Run both and you get the decoder's fluency with CTC's monotonicity acting as a stabiliser.

Streaming costs three percent

Making a Conformer stream takes two changes and only works if you do both. Attention gets chunked masking so a frame attends within its chunk plus some history. And the depthwise convolution has to become causal, left-padded only, because otherwise it leaks about 15 frames of future context and the attention mask you carefully wrote is fiction.

Train with the chunk size resampled every batch and one set of weights serves every latency operating point. Cost on my data: word error goes 0.607 to 0.627 for 640 ms of lookahead, roughly 3% relative.

Streaming usually gets discussed as a quality sacrifice. At this scale it's a rounding error, and the reason to pick an architecture that streams by construction is that retrofitting means changing the convolution, which means retraining.

Two ways my evaluation lied to me

My held-out set shared 39 of 39 speakers with training. Every word error rate I'd quoted was speaker-dependent, optimistic, and not comparable to any published number. Held out has to mean held out along the axis you care about, and for recognition that's speakers.

Then checkpoint averaging made things worse, which surprised me because it's folklore that it helps — averaging the last five gave 0.659 against a best single of 0.612. Averaging assumes the checkpoints orbit one basin. Mine spanned a run that went from 0.86 to 0.61, so they were in different basins and the average landed between them. I average the best K by held-out error now and keep it only if it wins.

What the numbers are worth

Whisper base.en gets 0.033 word error against my 0.607. It also saw 680,000 hours of audio against my 100. That's a 165,000× data advantage and quoting my number next to theirs without it would be dishonest.

More useful comparison: "the gap is data" turns out to be false as a general claim. For the VAD the gap really was data, and fixing the data fixed the model. For synthesis it was architecture. For recognition it's data, architecture, recipe and inference all at once, and the only way I found out which was which was changing one at a time.

I still don't have a good instinct for which one it'll be on a new problem. Maybe that only comes from doing it more times than I have.
