This is how to build the listening half of a voice agent without calling anyone's API: features, voice activity detection, endpointing, and the recognizer itself. By the end you should know what each stage is actually solving, which design choices matter, and which ones you can copy from a reference recipe and stop thinking about.

I'll give you the numbers from my own runs, because a recommendation with no measurement behind it is just an opinion.

The first thing to internalise is that these four boxes are four different kinds of problem, and the tools don't transfer between them. Denoising is signal processing. VAD is binary classification. Endpointing is policy with no model in it. Recognition is sequence transduction with an alignment problem underneath. Most of the time I got stuck, it was because I'd reached for the tool that belongs to the box next door.

Features: build the log-mel yourself once

Everything downstream eats log-mel spectrograms, so compute one by hand exactly once: frame the signal, window it, FFT, map onto the mel scale, take the log. Twenty minutes of work and you stop treating the input as magic.

The practical thing to take away: framing parameters are part of your model, not tuning knobs. I reused a 512/256 overlap-add shell for a component trained at 512/128 — it looked harmless, and it took a 19 dB signal-to-noise ratio down to 6 dB. Pick n_fft, window and hop once, write them somewhere central, and make every stage read from there.

VAD: your data is the model

A frame-level speech detector needs about 55,000 parameters. At that size you can stop blaming architecture for anything. Clarifying, in a way: every problem you have is now a data problem.

Mine reported P(speech) = 1.0 on room noise with nobody talking. Same architecture, same training loop, corrected data, and the same input scores 0.04.

Do this before you train anything Write the evaluation set first, and make it the specific things you expect to break: digital silence, mains hum, babble, speech recorded too quietly. Mine took ten minutes and then chose which checkpoint shipped — the best hard-case score was 0.806 at step 2750 while the last step of the run scored 0.699. Without a targeted set you'd ship the final checkpoint and never know.

Endpointing: the stage that should have no model

Endpointing answers "have they finished talking?" and I'd argue against putting a model here. It's a state machine over VAD output plus a hangover timer, and all the content lives in the policy.

Two layers work much better than one. The acoustic layer tells you a pause happened. It does not tell you the turn ended, because people pause mid-sentence constantly. So add a linguistic layer on top: if the utterance sounds finished, commit after about 300 ms; if it trails off on "and", "so", "because", "um", wait around 950 ms for more.

Two constants that matter more than they look. A redemption window of roughly 320 ms stops a breath from ending someone's turn. A pre-speech pad of around 220 ms keeps the onset consonant the detector would otherwise clip — without it "sixty" arrives as "ixty" often enough to be a real problem.

One architectural consequence, and it shapes your whole system: barge-in means detecting speech while the agent is talking. VAD is sub-millisecond so it can run inline, but recognition and synthesis take hundreds of milliseconds. If either of them runs on your event loop, the agent cannot hear you while it speaks. Decide your threading model here, not later.

The alignment problem, and how CTC dodges it

Here's the core difficulty of recognition. You have 400 audio frames and three characters, and nothing tells you which frames belong to which character. Hand-labelling that at scale isn't happening.

CTC's move is to refuse the question. You define a many-to-one collapse — merge repeated symbols, drop blanks, then sum the probability of every frame labelling that collapses to your target. A forward-backward recursion makes that sum tractable. You never commit to one alignment; you score all of them at once.

The blank symbol does two jobs that are easy to conflate, and understanding both is most of understanding CTC:

  • it lets the model emit nothing at a frame it's unsure about, and that's what makes the many-to-one collapse work at all;
  • it separates genuine repeats. Without a blank between them, "hello" collapses to "helo".

Here's what a CTC model looks like when it's working acoustically and has no language model. Expect this early on:

hyp: he inl san er mianthes tlen kis roint the stieia sord meai wen
ref: but in less than five minutes the staircase groaned beneath an
     extraordinary weight

Rhythm's roughly right. Syllable count's roughly right. What's absent is any notion that English words are a finite set — and that's structural, not a bug. CTC assumes conditional independence between output frames given the audio, and that's precisely a statement that it has no language model. If your early output looks like this, the model is learning; it just has nothing telling it which sounds are words.

Choosing your architecture

The three real options, and when each is right:

ApproachLanguage modelStreams?Use when
CTCnone, needs an external onetriviallycheapest, and a good first build
RNN-Tinternal, over output historyyes, monotonicthe production default for streaming
Attention encoder-decoderstrongestbadly, and can hallucinateoffline accuracy, e.g. Whisper

Hybrid CTC/attention is what I'd recommend building: one encoder, two heads. CTC's monotonicity acts as a stabiliser while the decoder supplies the language modelling, and each covers the other's structural weakness.

Swapping a BiGRU+CTC baseline for a Conformer with that hybrid setup, on identical audio, is the single biggest win available:

1.076 to 0.607word error, 44% relative
0.658 to 0.276character error, 58% relative
2.7xfaster inference
0extra training data

Two things there matter when you build it. The ×4 subsampling before the blocks makes everything 16× cheaper and costs you nothing, so do it first. And two thirds of each block is feed-forward — attention is the smallest learned part, the opposite of what the name suggests, and useful to know when you're deciding what to shrink.

Making it stream, for 3%

Streaming gets discussed as a quality sacrifice. At this scale it's a rounding error, and it takes two changes that only work together:

  • Chunked attention masking, so a frame attends within its chunk plus some history.
  • A causal depthwise convolution, left-padded only. Skip this and the conv leaks about 15 frames of future context, which makes the attention mask you carefully wrote a fiction.

Resample the chunk size every batch during training and one set of weights serves every latency operating point. Measured cost: word error goes 0.607 to 0.627 for 640 ms of lookahead, roughly 3% relative.

Pick an architecture that streams by construction even if you don't need it yet. Retrofitting means changing the convolution, and changing the convolution means retraining.

Things that will cost you a day each

These are the ones I'd want to have been told:

  • Warm up over ~15,000 steps, not 1,500. Too fast a rise locks CTC into the all-blank minimum and it doesn't come back. Mine collapsed to 100% blank frames, entropy 0.82 of a possible 6.93, decoding to an empty string — and neither the loss (flat, plausible) nor word error (pinned at 1.0, same as an untrained model) could see it. Log blank-frame fraction; it goes to 1.0 immediately.
  • Read the reference recipe before changing hyperparameters. I was about to spend three days shrinking a 1024 vocabulary. ESPnet uses 5,000 on the same corpus for 8.4% word error. The vocabulary was never the problem.
  • Hold out speakers, not utterances. My held-out set shared 39 of 39 speakers with training, so every number I'd quoted was speaker-dependent and not comparable with anything published.
  • Checkpoint averaging has a precondition. It assumes the checkpoints orbit one basin. Mine spanned 0.86 to 0.61, so averaging the last five gave 0.659 against a best single of 0.612. Average the best K by held-out error, and keep it only if it wins.
  • zero_infinity=True hides infeasible samples. If a target is longer than the encoder output, CTC is impossible and PyTorch substitutes a zero gradient instead of raising. A bad vocabulary silently trains on a fraction of your data.
  • Verify the GPU by using it. torch.cuda.is_available() returned True twice on machines where a convolution then failed. Run a real convolution in your bootstrap and look at the result.

What to expect at the end

My recognizer gets 0.607 word error. Whisper base.en gets 0.033. It also saw 680,000 hours against my 100, a 165,000× data advantage, and quoting one number next to the other without that ratio would be dishonest.

So don't build this to win. Build it because "the gap is data" turns out to be false as a general claim, and you can only find that out by moving one thing at a time. For the VAD the gap really was data. For synthesis it was architecture. For recognition it's data, architecture, recipe and inference all at once — and after this you'll be able to tell which one you're looking at, and that beats the model.
