Speech synthesis splits cleanly into two problems that fail in completely different ways, and most of the confusion I see probably comes from treating them as one. This walks through both: how text becomes a spectrogram, how a spectrogram becomes sound, and which decisions at each stage actually determine whether the output is intelligible.

Text to mel is a sequence problem: durations, prosody, pronunciation. Mel to waveform is a signal problem: inverting a transform that threw information away. Modern end-to-end systems fuse the two for quality, but you can't see why fusing helps until you've felt the seam, so build them separately first.

Why synthesis is harder than recognition

If you've built a recognizer, you've met the alignment problem: 400 audio frames, three characters, and nothing saying which frames belong to which character. CTC handles it by refusing to choose, summing over every possible alignment.

Run it backwards and that escape route disappears.

RecognitionSynthesis
you're given400 frames, 3 characters19 characters
you producethe text250 frames, and you decide it's 250
the alignment ishidden in the audio, waitingnot there at all, you invent it
sum over all of them?yes, that's CTCno, you emit one sequence

That last row is the whole difficulty. CTC stays agnostic because it only scores a target you already hold. A synthesizer produces the output, so at every step it commits: is the "o" in "go" finished, or does it hold another 40 ms? Get that wrong repeatedly and you don't get a slightly wrong transcript. You get speech that stutters, skips syllables, or never stops.

The acoustic model, and the four tricks it needs

Tacotron's answer is to generate mel frames one step at a time, using attention over the text at each step to answer "which character am I on?". The attention is the alignment, built as it goes.

Four things make that trainable, and you need all four:

TrickWhat it doesWithout it
Teacher forcingfeed the true previous frame during trainingearly training compounds its own garbage
Prenet dropout, on at inference toostops the decoder copying the previous frame forwardfluent, confident mumble, uncorrelated with the text

The second one deserves a note because it looks like a bug you'd fix. Leaving dropout active at inference is deliberate: the previous-frame path is so informative that the decoder will happily copy it forward and ignore the text entirely. Turn it off and you get speech that sounds completely fluent and has nothing to do with what you asked for.

Watch the alignment map, not the loss

This is the single most useful thing I can tell you about training a synthesizer.

Mel L1 loss falls early just by predicting blurry average speech. Mine went 0.81 to 0.24, smoothly, over 3,000 steps — the shape of curve you screenshot and feel good about — while the model learned essentially nothing. The free-running attention map was a smear with no diagonal. Generation cut out at 2.2 seconds on an 82-character sentence.

A diagonal forming in the attention map means it's working. A smear means it isn't. The loss cannot distinguish those two states, so plot the alignment every few hundred steps and look at it.

If the diagonal won't form, the fix usually isn't more data. Maybe eventually, but not yet. Guided attention adds a penalty mean(A · W) where W[t,n] = 1 − exp(−(n/N − t/T)² / 2g²), taxing attention mass far from the diagonal and leaving mass near it free. It's a prior saying "time moves forward", not a constraint, and with enough data you'd drop it. With 19 training utterances it took alignment from never forming to forming in hundreds of steps.

Scoring it without a listening panel

Mel L1 says nothing about intelligibility. If you've already built a recognizer, use it: transcribe the synthesizer's output and measure character error against the text it was asked to say.

Build that judge early, because quality oscillates while the loss slides steadily downward. Mine swung between 0.96 and 0.61 character error across a run with three separate regressions, and picked step 8,000 of 10,000. Taking the final checkpoint would have shipped something clearly worse for no reason except that it came last.

Griffin-Lim is a ceiling, and you should plan to leave it

Computing a log-mel throws the phase away. Griffin-Lim recovers a phase by exploiting frame overlap: guess, inverse transform, re-measure, keep your magnitude, repeat about 60 times. After that, magnitude and phase are self-consistent.

Self-consistent isn't correct. Many phase fields are consistent with the same magnitude spectrogram, and the one Griffin-Lim converges to generally isn't the one a speaker produced. That mismatch is the phasey, metallic, slightly underwater quality you hear in every Griffin-Lim sample.

Why this gap is different Almost everywhere else in a speech stack, more data helps. Here it can't, at any quantity, because the limitation isn't in a learned component at all. Griffin-Lim has zero parameters. Train your acoustic model on ten thousand hours and the output is still phasey. If your synthesis sounds robotic, replace the vocoder before you collect more speech.

The insight behind the spectral family is that making a waveform was never the hard part. The inverse STFT does that exactly, for free, and has done since 1977. Knowing the phase was the hard part. So predict the phase, run the exact inverse, and stop paying a network to rediscover arithmetic.

For anything targeting a laptop CPU inside a turn-taking latency budget, I'd take the spectral one. Two orders of magnitude less compute per second of audio, and it clears Griffin-Lim trained on a multi-resolution STFT loss alone, so adversarial training becomes a later upgrade instead of a prerequisite.

No attention anywhere in it. A vocoder needs a receptive field of a few tens of milliseconds, enough to place a glottal pulse, not a sentence. Convolutions supply that far more cheaply, and that's probably why every production vocoder you'll meet is convolutional.

Match its STFT grid to the one your features already use — n_fft 512, window 400, hop 160 in my case — and the mels your acoustic model emits drop straight in with no retraining.

Pick a metric that can see what you care about

I scored my neural vocoder against Griffin-Lim on multi-resolution STFT distance. Griffin-Lim won.

It won because multi-resolution STFT is a magnitude metric, and magnitude consistency is literally the objective Griffin-Lim optimises. I'd picked a scoring function that measures the one thing the baseline is built to maximise. Adding SI-SDR, time-domain and so phase-sensitive, flipped it immediately: the neural model was 5.4 dB better.

Before trusting any comparison, ask whether your metric measures the thing your baseline optimises. If it does, you've built a tautology.

If you want it in your own voice

Cloning is mostly a data-collection problem, and it's the one step here you can't parallelise, buy or download. An hour of speech is around 900 takes. At ten seconds of friction per take that's two and a half hours of pure overhead, which is how most voice datasets end up abandoned.

So build the recording tool properly, and design it to remove decisions:

  • Hands free. Press return, read the sentence, stop talking, and your VAD ends the take. No second keypress across 900 sentences.
  • Check every take before it counts. Clipping, noise floor, silence padding, and whether a transcription matches the prompt. Catching a bad take costs five seconds now and a day after training.
  • Resumable at any instant, because nobody records 900 sentences in one sitting.
  • Balance the corpus by greedy n-gram coverage when choosing prompts, so an hour of speech covers the sounds you need instead of the sentences you happened to pick.

Verifying a voice is a voice

If you go further and build speaker embeddings, the important design decision is that the training objective and the reported metric are deliberately different tasks. You train a classifier over known speakers, but you report equal error rate on speakers the model has never heard, scored by cosine distance on a fixed trial list. Training accuracy hitting 100% only means it memorised 32 voices.

And check your confidence interval before believing any A/B. A held-out split of 8 speakers gives an equal error rate with a bootstrap interval several points wide — wider than most of the differences you'd want to test, like angular margin against plain softmax. Print the interval, the median across evaluations and the best checkpoint separately, so they can't be quietly confused.

On a split that small, the honest answer to "does the angular margin help?" is that the experiment can't tell you. Knowing which of your results are real is the part that transfers to the next project.
