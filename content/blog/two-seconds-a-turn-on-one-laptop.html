Vela is an assistant that lives in a process which does not stop when you close the window. She sees the room through the camera, hears you, answers out loud, and can do things. Everything except the language model runs on the laptop.

The interesting parts are not the models. They are off the shelf. The interesting parts are a latency budget that has to be defended stage by stage, a feedback loop that made her talk to herself indefinitely, and a 90 times speed gap between two text-to-speech systems that turned out to be architectural rather than anything I could tune.

1The rule that shapes everything

Every companion-style AI project dies the same death. The agent has nothing to actually do, so it fabricates an inner life, and you feel the lie inside ninety seconds.

So the rule is no theater. If she did nothing, she says she did nothing. If she does not know, she says so. If the camera is muted, she says it is muted rather than describing the room. Nothing in her notebook is generated prose; every entry is an artifact with a timestamp behind it.

That sounds like a design philosophy and it is really an engineering constraint, because it rules out the cheap version of every feature. Her situation summary is read out of the database each turn, which is why asking what step her model reached returns 5600, a real measurement from her ledger, instead of a plausible number.

2The turn budget

mic ──▶ Silero VAD ──▶ Whisper (MLX) ──▶ brain ──▶ Kokoro ──▶ speaker ~1ms/frame ~0.3s 0.67s 0.2s 48kHz local local, Metal remote local, Metal One turn, end of your sentence to start of hers: about 2 seconds

Measured on an M4 MacBook Air with 16 GB, and every number here came off a run on that machine rather than an estimate. Voice activity detection is Silero v5 on 512 sample frames and costs nothing worth measuring. Transcription is whisper-base.en through MLX on the GPU at about 0.3 seconds. Synthesis is Kokoro-82M at 13.2 times realtime. The only remote call is the language model, at 0.67 seconds.

Endpointing costs between 300 and 950 milliseconds, which makes it the second most expensive stage in the whole loop, and it contains no model at all.

3Turn detection is two layers, not one

Silero tells you a pause happened. It does not tell you the turn ended, and those are very different claims, because people pause mid-sentence constantly. An assistant that treats every acoustic gap as your turn ending interrupts you for a living.

So there is a second layer on top of the acoustic endpoint, and it is linguistic rather than acoustic. If the utterance sounds finished, commit after 300 milliseconds. If it trails off on "and", "so", "because" or "um", wait 950 milliseconds for more. If more speech arrives, append it and re-evaluate.

Two smaller constants matter as much as the big ones. A redemption window of 320 milliseconds stops a breath from ending your turn. A pre-speech pad of 220 milliseconds keeps the onset consonant that the detector would otherwise clip, which is the difference between hearing "sixty" and hearing "ixty".

4She answered herself, forever

Speakers leak into the microphone. That sentence is obvious written down and I did not design for it, so here is what the log looked like:

(tts 1.97s for 2.4s of audio)
(cut off)
(cut off after 0.3s)
you: ONE            <- her own voice
vela: One.

She speaks. The speaker output leaks into the microphone. Voice activity detection fires on her own voice. Barge-in kills playback about 300 milliseconds in. Whisper transcribes the leak. She answers it. That is a closed loop with no exit, and every individual component behaved exactly as specified.

Where the fix has to live Not in any component. Each one was correct. The loop only exists at the level of the orchestrator, which is the piece with no paper behind it. The microphone is now ignored while she is speaking, plus a 350 millisecond tail for room reverb. Barge-in comes back if you are on headphones, because then the premise of the problem is gone.

5Match commands before you reach for the model

Opening an app, pausing music, setting a timer and reading the time are all matched by regular expressions before any model call happens. That looks primitive next to routing everything through a language model, and it is the right call for three reasons: those commands answer instantly, they answer deterministically, and they cannot be talked out of doing the right thing. Anything unmatched falls through to conversation.

The lesson generalises past assistants. The parts of a system where the correct behaviour is fully specified do not benefit from a model, and putting one there converts a guarantee into a probability.

6Telling the model what it cannot do

Small models fail a specific way: asked about a capability they do not have, they answer as if they do. Asked what is in your calendar, a 3B model will cheerfully invent a meeting.

The prompt now states explicitly that she has no calendar, email, files, battery, location, browser history or screen access. That single addition moved the small models from 1 out of 4 to 4 out of 5 on a set of hallucination traps. They had not been lying so much as under-instructed: nothing in the context said those capabilities were absent, so the most probable continuation was the one where they exist.

7Why she does not speak in my voice yet

The goal was for her to answer in my voice, from a 32 second clean reference recording. She does not, and the reason is the most useful thing I measured all month.

34.9sto speak 4.9s of audio
81.3%of it in one stage
4.4tokens per second
25needed for realtime

Profiling the two stages separately was the whole investigation. The vocoder takes 4.8 seconds and is fine. The token generator takes 28.4 seconds. It is one stage, and it is 5.7 times too slow, which means every optimisation aimed anywhere else is wasted effort.

What that stage does is generate speech autoregressively: a 0.5B transformer emitting discrete speech tokens at 25 per second of audio, one at a time, each conditioned on all the previous ones. A five second reply is around 125 sequential forward passes and none of them can be parallelised.

Kokoro is fast because it is not autoregressive. It predicts the whole mel spectrogram in essentially one shot. That architectural difference is the entire 90 times gap between them, and no amount of tuning closes it.

8A model three times larger, running four times faster

The damning comparison is between two models on the same machine.

ModelParametersRuntimeRate
Qwen2.51.5BMLXabout 20 tokens/s
Chatterbox T30.5BPyTorch / MPS4.4 tokens/s

Three times the parameters, four and a half times the speed. Per parameter, MLX is roughly 13 times more efficient here than PyTorch's MPS backend.

The reason is what autoregressive decoding actually is. It is memory-bandwidth bound, not compute bound: each token requires reading the whole weight matrix out of memory, and the arithmetic performed on it is trivial by comparison. MLX exploits unified memory and avoids per-kernel launch overhead. MPS does neither well. This particular model also runs classifier-free guidance, which means two forward passes per token and therefore double the bandwidth cost.

So the bottleneck is not the model size, and it is not the laptop. It is the runtime, which is a much more actionable conclusion than "clone mode is slow" and I would never have got there without splitting the profile by stage.

Ranked by expected gain, the fix is to port that stage to MLX, which would put it near 2.4 times realtime. Before that, there is a cheap one worth more than it looks: her replies repeat constantly, so pre-synthesising the fifty most common ones in my voice and hash-keying them means you hear my voice most of the time at zero latency, with the fast model as the fallback for novel sentences.

9Three of five failed on dependency rot

Five approaches to voice cloning were tried. One works well and is too slow. One garbled its output. The other three never got far enough to be judged on quality: one pins a library version that removes a dependency the working synthesizer needs, one calls a model-hub API that no longer exists, and one has a README telling you to go use a different project.

That is the state of this corner of the ecosystem right now, and it is worth budgeting for. When three of five candidates fail on packaging rather than on merit, evaluation time is mostly environment time, and the right move is to timebox each candidate to the question "does it install and run at all" before you listen to a single sample.

10Always discard the first run

My first benchmark reported 0.05 times realtime on short text and 0.29 on longer text. Longer text taking proportionally less time is impossible, which is what made the result readable: warmup had landed entirely on the first call. Warm, it measures 0.59.

Thermal state moves these numbers meaningfully too, on a fanless machine that has been working for an hour. A benchmark on a laptop is measuring the laptop's current mood as much as the code, and the honest way to report it is to say which run, how warm, and how many.

She currently sits at phase six of six, where she can propose an edit to her own code, branch it, run her tests against it, canary it, and merge or revert. Her research program is paused at my request, which she will tell you if you ask, because the alternative would be theater.
