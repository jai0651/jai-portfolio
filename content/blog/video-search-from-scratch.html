The goal: upload a two-hour lecture, ask "when was backpropagation explained?" or "find every segment with a red car", and get an answer back with the timestamps and frames that support it.

This is how the pieces fit together, which decisions actually matter, and how to build an evaluation set that can tell you whether any of it works. The architecture generalises to most retrieval systems over time-based media.

Start by splitting the system in half

The single most important structural decision is recognising that you're building two systems with opposite constraints, and keeping them apart.

Ingestion is a batch job. It can be slow, it runs once per video, and its binding constraint is memory — a two-hour video will happily exhaust whatever you give it. Query is interactive, runs constantly, and its binding constraint is latency. Optimising them together gets you a system that's mediocre at both.

Decoding: get your timestamps from the container

Your first decision is how to represent a frame, and there's a trap here worth avoiding.

The tempting design carries a frame index and computes time as index / fps wherever it's needed. Simpler, no time-base arithmetic, and on a normal test file the drift measures 0.00 ms against the container's own timestamps.

It's correct for constant frame rate video and nothing else. Screen recorders emit frames when the screen changes, so a QuickTime or Zoom capture at "30 fps" is an average, and index-derived time drifts without bound. Since the whole deliverable is a timestamp somebody clicks, the failure mode is a confident citation pointing at the wrong moment — and no test catches it unless the test checks the number.

Take time from the container's presentation timestamps, and write one test asserting the two agree on a file you know is constant-rate.

Frame selection: where your compute budget goes

You cannot encode every frame of a two-hour video, so this stage decides your whole accuracy-versus-cost frontier. The options, roughly in order of sophistication: uniform sampling, motion-aware sampling, shot-boundary detection, and novelty-based coreset selection.

One shortcut that looks free and isn't: encoders insert keyframes at scene cuts, and a keyframe scan costs about 0.03× a full decode, so you'd think it hands you shot boundaries for nothing. On an authored test video where I knew the answer, x264 found one boundary of four. It broke its regular three-second cadence exactly once, at the hard cut. The dissolve produced no keyframe at all, and two more boundaries only looked found because the cadence happened to land near them.

So treat keyframes as a high-precision, low-recall candidate generator: a broken cadence means something, silence means nothing. Useful as a prior, not as an answer.

Worth knowing that the boundary it missed was the dissolve, and commercial scene-cut heuristics fail on dissolves for the same reason — the change per frame is genuinely small. When a well-tuned production system fails the way yours does, the difficulty is in the problem, and you can stop trying to tune your way out.

Three retrieval channels, and fusing them

A video carries at least three searchable signals, and they cover different questions:

  • Transcript from ASR with word-level timestamps, chunked into temporal segments. Chunk size is a real hyperparameter, and boundary loss is real: an answer that straddles two chunks is retrievable from neither.
  • Visual embeddings from CLIP-style encoders over selected frames. This is what answers "find the red car".
  • On-screen text via OCR, deduplicated across near-identical slides. On lecture content this one outperforms the visual channel pretty regularly, and it's the one people skip.

Fusing them is mostly about score normalisation, because the three channels produce scores on incomparable scales. Reciprocal rank fusion is probably the right default: it uses only the rank, so you don't have to calibrate anything. Try weighted score fusion after you have a benchmark, not before.

Chunks are not answers

Retrieval gives you chunks. A user wants an interval. Reranking is where you merge adjacent hits, expand to neighbours, and rescore the merged span with a cross-encoder.

Then build the evidence packet with a token budget before anything reaches a VLM: interleaved frames and text, trimmed to fit, with every piece carrying its timestamp so the answer can cite it. Letting the model see unbounded context is how you get slow, expensive answers that still can't point at anything.

Build the benchmark before you tune anything

This is the part I'd tell anyone to do first, and it's probably the part everyone defers.

You need your own typed evaluation set: questions labelled by what kind they are (speech, visual, on-screen text, temporal), each with a tight ground-truth interval. Then every module gets scored on it. Without this you're tuning by vibes, and with it you find things like these three, all of which were invisible in normal use:

Give a perfect input, demand a perfect score My timestamp metric reported 0.60 s mean error and IoU 0.73, and I wrote a whole paragraph about what that meant. Then a test fed it an oracle transcript — authored text, authored timings, zero error by construction — and it scored 0.64 s. The metric was selecting which hypothesis words to score using the predicted timings, which is circular, and its ±1 s window was wider than the 0.45 s gap between sentences, so every span absorbed its neighbours. Aligning by text instead: 0.15 s mean error, IoU 0.88 to 0.93. The real result was four times better than the one I'd published.

Measure retrieval and answerability separately. My per-modality table claimed visual questions were impossible for a speech-only system while printing hit@5 of 1.000 for them. Both were true about different things: the narration says "an object travels across the screen" without ever naming its colour, so retrieval finds the right moment and the answer isn't there. Localisation succeeded, answerability failed, and no metric I had could tell them apart. Add one that asks whether the retrieved text actually contains the answer — mine reads 0.778 for speech questions and exactly 0.000 for every other type.

Test the benchmark itself. My temporal questions scored hit@5 of 1.000, and so did random retrieval, exactly 1.000. I'd given those questions ground-truth intervals spanning from one shot to nearly the end of the video, so any chunk overlaps and every retrieval counts as correct. Assert that chance-level performance on a question falls below some threshold. Tight intervals are the only thing that makes temporal overlap mean anything.

Diagnostics that video specifically needs

Two that generic retrieval tutorials won't mention, because they only bite on temporal media.

Near-duplicate fraction. Consecutive frames of a static slide sit about a millionth of a unit apart in embedding space. That breaks anything assuming a continuous density: my intrinsic-dimension estimator returned 0.3, which isn't a possible value, because one distance ratio reached 345,901 and dominated the sum. 75 of 89 frames had a near twin. Exclude pairs below a floor, and promote near-duplicate fraction to a first-class diagnostic — the quantity that broke the estimator turned out to be one of the most important facts about the data.

Sweep the dimension before you believe an ordering. I measured embedding contrast across three data regimes at 512 dimensions, decided the ordering was a law, and asserted it in a test. It failed immediately at 256, where two of the three swap places. The regimes are controlled by different parameters and don't scale with dimension the same way.

Environment, briefly

Two things that cost an afternoon and have nothing to do with retrieval. Pin one Python minor version back from newest — wheel availability lags releases by six to twelve months and the long tail lags further. And if your test suite aborts during a torch import only when two particular files run together, that's two OpenMP runtimes in one process (torch ships one, FAISS ships another) and not a logic bug. Import order won't fix it; the abort happens on initialisation, so reordering only changes which library dies.

What to build from scratch, and what not to

I wrote four components explicitly and then measured them against the production alternative behind the same interface: a mini-CLIP, the nearest neighbour index built up from brute force through IVF to HNSW, a shot detector, and a spatiotemporal video encoder.

The scratch versions lose, and they're meant to. Ship FAISS and a pretrained encoder. But you can't reason about a retrieval stack you've never implemented, and every problem above was found by reasoning about the stack, not by writing a layer. Maybe build one of them, not all four, and spend the rest of your time on the evaluation set.
